Chapter 1

A model can be confident
and still be wrong.

Machine-learning models give a confidence score alongside every prediction — like saying it is 90% sure of its answer.

When the model says 90% confident, it should be right about 90% of the time. That is what well-calibrated confidence means.

But what happens when the same model is given data from a completely different telescope? Does the confidence score still mean anything?

Research Question
"Can we trust the confidence score given by an ML model when it classifies variable stars from a different astronomical survey?"

This project tests that question using NASA TESS and ESA Gaia DR3.

Ideal
90% → ~90% correct
Confidence ≈ correctness
vs
What we found
90% → ~49% agreement
Confidence ≠ correctness
Chapter 2

Eclipsing binary stars.

This project studies a particular kind of star system: eclipsing binaries. Before anything else, you need to know what they are.

Two stars orbiting each other, viewed edge-on. Watch the brightness curve below.

Two stars orbit each other. If the orbital plane is roughly aligned with our line of sight, one star can pass in front of the other — as seen from Earth.

When that happens, the combined brightness we observe drops. That dip in brightness is an eclipse. The characteristic pattern of dips over time is called a light curve.

Detached (label 0) — the stars are well separated. The light curve shows sharp, distinct dips with a flat section between them.
Contact-like (label 1) — the stars are very close or physically touching. The brightness varies continuously with no flat section.
How we defined labels for TESS

The TESS catalog gives each star a shape score from 0 to 1 based on its light curve. We used a threshold of 0.5: below 0.5 → label 0 (detached-like), at or above 0.5 → label 1 (contact-like).

Two telescopes. Two data environments.

Both NASA TESS and ESA Gaia are space missions that observe stars. But they work differently, cover different parts of the sky, and produce data in different formats.

Important: Gaia does not classify stars as detached or contact. It assigns a mathematical model type (like TWOGAUSSIANS or ELLIPSOIDAL). We converted these into proxy labels. When we checked a subset of these labels against an independent catalog (VSX), agreement was only 39.2%. So our Gaia results show agreement with proxy labels — not physical ground truth.

Chapters 8 & 9

Was confidence calibrated?

A model is well calibrated when its confidence matches how often it is actually correct.

If it says 70% confident, it should be right about 70% of the time. If it says 90%, about 90% of the time.

The reliability diagram shows this. Confidence level runs across the bottom. How often it was actually right runs up the side. A perfectly calibrated model follows the diagonal line.

90% confident → ~90% correct ✓ calibrated
90% confident → ~49% agreement ✗ not calibrated
TESS: Stays close to the diagonal. At ≥90% confidence: 96.6% correct.
Gaia: Falls far below the diagonal. In the 0.70–0.80 bin: only 9.2% proxy-label agreement.
The y-axis for Gaia reflects agreement with proxy labels — not physical correctness.

Brier score: how close were the probabilities?

The Brier score measures how far the model's probabilities were from what actually happened. Lower is better. If you guessed randomly, you would score about 0.25. Higher than 0.25 means worse than random.

Chapter 11

Why did confidence become unreliable?

We investigated. Here are the differences we found between the two data environments.

Orbital periods look different

Gaia contains many more short-period systems than the TESS training data. Each bar is scaled relative to that survey's own peak (normalized to peak).

TESS median: 2.07 days · 30.3% below 1 day Gaia median: 0.458 days · 66.9% below 1 day

Eclipse durations also look different

In Gaia, eclipses are typically much wider than what the model saw during training. Each bar is scaled relative to that survey's own peak (normalized to peak).

TESS median: 0.079 phase fraction Gaia median: 0.284 — 3.6× wider

These differences may affect how the model behaves on Gaia.

Removing eclipse duration did not fix the problem. In fact, mean Gaia confidence increased (the model became even more overconfident — meaning its confidence rose while its agreement with the labels did not). The calibration gap barely changed.

Restricting Gaia to longer-period systems helped slightly — but the confidence problem remained substantial.

"The confidence problem remained substantial after these controlled changes. No tested variation resolved it."

Chapter 14

What this project does not prove.

Honesty matters in research. Here is what we cannot claim.

✗
Random Forest is inherently unreliable.
✗
Cross-survey transfer is generally impossible.
✗
Eclipse duration is the sole cause of the confidence shift.
✗
Gaia data is incorrect — Gaia proxy-label errors and model errors cannot be separated.
✗
This finding applies to all surveys or all classification tasks.
✗
No single causal explanation was established.

The Gaia labels came from a mathematical fitting algorithm, not from physical observations. An independent check showed only 39.2% agreement. Some apparent errors may reflect label disagreements rather than model failures.

Only two features were used. Only one model type was tested. The Gaia sample (489 sources) is enough for a first study, but some confidence ranges had very few predictions, making those results less reliable.

The Finding

Confidence should be evaluated,
not assumed.

The model looked reliable on TESS.

On Gaia, its confidence became much less reliable.

The experiments did not identify one single cause.

The model worked well on TESS data.

When we moved it to Gaia, high confidence no longer matched correctness as closely.

This shows why model confidence should be checked when data changes.
